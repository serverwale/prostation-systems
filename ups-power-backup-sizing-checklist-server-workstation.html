UPS Sizing for a Server or Workstation: A Five-Step Checklist

A server or workstation has no built-in battery, so a power cut or a bad voltage dip can corrupt a database, interrupt a RAID rebuild or ruin a long training run. Sizing the UPS is a short calculation, not guesswork. This is a quick checklist; the full guide is ProStation's power backup and UPS sizing guide for servers and workstations. The kVA ranges below are general planning starting points, not a quote, so measure your own configuration before you buy.

The five steps

StepWhat to do
1. Find the real drawUse the sustained watts the machine pulls under your workload, measured with a plug-in power meter, not the power supply's rating. An 850 W supply does not draw 850 W all day. Ask the builder for the measured draw of your exact configuration.
2. Convert watts to VAVA = watts ÷ power factor. Server supplies with active power factor correction sit near 0.9 to 0.95, but many budget UPS units are rated on a 0.6 to 0.7 assumption, so a "1000 VA" unit may deliver only about 600 to 700 W. Always read the watt rating.
3. Add headroomAdd roughly 25 to 30 percent for the switch-on current spike and for future upgrades such as another drive, more RAM or a second GPU.
4. Choose the runtimeFor most single servers 10 to 15 minutes is enough to trigger a clean, automatic shutdown, which is what actually protects the data. Plan longer only if the workload cannot stop, and then expect a bigger battery bank or a generator.
5. Choose the typeLine-interactive units suit a basic file or application server. An online double-conversion UPS gives clean power with zero transfer time and is the safer choice for a hypervisor, a database or a long unattended job.

Worked example (illustrative arithmetic)

Suppose a meter shows a sustained 400 W under load. With 30 percent headroom you need about 520 W of real output. A UPS sold as 1000 VA but rated at 0.6 power factor delivers only 600 W, which leaves almost no margin, while the same machine on a 1000 VA unit rated at 0.9 power factor has 900 W available. The VA number alone told you nothing; the watt rating did. This is a method illustration, not a recommendation for any specific model.

Do not forget these

  • Network gear. A protected server is useless mid-outage if the switch or router goes dark. A small 600 VA to 1 kVA UPS for the network rack is cheap insurance.
  • Automatic shutdown. The UPS only buys time. Configure the shutdown software so the machine powers down cleanly before the battery ends.
  • Redundant supplies. Two power supplies protect against a supply failure, not a grid failure; read the redundant PSU guide for what each one covers.
  • Data protection. Clean power complements, not replaces, RAID and backups; see RAID levels explained.
  • Heat. A UPS and a loaded workstation in a small room add heat; see why workstations overheat.
  • Cost of downtime. Decide how much protection is worth paying for using the downtime cost and warranty guide.

Frequently asked questions

  • How many kVA UPS does a small business server need? — It depends on the real sustained draw and the runtime you want, but a single entry or mid-range tower server typically needs 1 to 2.5 kVA for a clean 10 to 15 minute shutdown window. Measure the draw of your configuration first.
  • Should I size the UPS from the power supply rating? — No. The supply rating is its maximum capacity, not what the server draws. Size from the measured sustained draw under your workload, then add roughly 25 to 30 percent headroom.
  • Is a line-interactive UPS enough for a server? — It is fine for a basic file or application server. For virtualisation, databases and long unattended jobs, an online double-conversion UPS is the safer choice because it has zero transfer time.
  • How long should the server run on battery? — For most single-server setups, 10 to 15 minutes is enough for an automatic clean shutdown. Plan for longer only if the workload cannot tolerate downtime, which needs a bigger battery bank or a generator.
  • Does a multi-GPU server need a bigger UPS? — Yes. GPUs under sustained load are usually the largest power draw in a high-end build, so plan for roughly 2.5 to 3.5 kVA or more on an online UPS and confirm against the exact GPU count.
